All itemsLess food and energy

Core removes food and energy — not because they do not count, but because they are volatile enough to swamp the signal in any single month. Both lines break at October 2025, which was never published: the shutdown stopped field collection, and BLS priced only the few items it could take from administrative sources. One missing month costs exactly one month of the twelve-month change here — and will cost another in October 2026, when the hole becomes the base rather than the current value.

The identical decomposition on the monthly change, same colours, same order. This is the pair to read when the two directions disagree: a month can add to the monthly rate while the annual rate falls, because the month dropping out of the twelve-month window twelve months back was hotter still. Table 4CPI, month and year togetherPercent, s.a.

Bars are the month; the line is the year.

Month on monthYear over year

Bars are the monthly change, the ink line the twelve-month change on the same axis. The year figure is nothing more than twelve of the bars compounded, so the line turns only when a run of bars sits above or below the ones dropping out of the window a year back. A single hot month barely moves it; three in a row do.

Table 9Core services, month and yearPercent, s.a.

Core services, led by shelter.

Month on monthYear over year

60.2% of the basket in one line, and the reason core inflation moves slowly. Shelter alone is more than half of it, and shelter is measured from a rolling rent sample, so the monthly bars here are the most autocorrelated on the page: this month’s reading is the best available guess at next month’s.

Table 10What makes up the CPI, latest monthPercentage points of the 12-month change

What each part added to the annual rate.

Not inflation rates — contributions: each item’s own twelve-month change multiplied by its share of the basket, so the four add up to the headline rather than merely sitting beside it. A complete partition, weights summing to 100.000%; the residual against the headline is a few hundredths of a point, and Table 2 shows it as daylight. Analyst-derived.

Table 11And what each is worthPercent of the basket

Each part’s share of the basket.

The weights behind the bars to the left, on the same four parts. Read the two together: a part with a small weight outranks a large one only by running at many times its rate, which is how energy, at 7.4% of the basket, can carry as much of the headline as food. Weights drift with relative prices every month by BLS’s own rule, so these are current, not a fixed table.

Table 14And what each is worth in corePercent of core

Each half’s share of core.

A different denominator from Table 11, and the numbers are not interchangeable: core goods are 18.8% of the CPI but 23.8% of core, because core excludes the food and energy that dilute it. The two halves of core as rates rather than contributions. Goods and services inflate for different reasons and on different clocks: goods track supply chains and the dollar, services track wages and rents. They separated sharply after 2021 and have not converged since.

The weights behind Table 17, and the reason its ranking looks the way it does. Shelter is 35.3% of the index on its own — nine times gasoline — so an unremarkable rate on shelter outranks a dramatic one on apparel. These twelve carry 79.45% between them.

Table 19And what they are worth in corePercent of core

Each item’s share of core.

The same items measured against core instead, which is a smaller basket: each weight is its CPI share divided by core’s own 79.047%. Shelter is 35.3% of the CPI and 44.7% of core. Food, energy, gasoline, electricity and utility gas are not in core at all and so have no bar here — the list is seven items, not twelve, and they carry 74.35% of core.

Table 20The same items, over four yearsPercentage points, 12-month change, 48 months

Item contributions over four years.

The ranking in Table 17 is one column of this. Contributions rather than rates, so cells are directly comparable across items: a dark cell is a large push on the index, not merely a large price move on something small. Four years reaches back into the 2022 peak, where gasoline and used cars ran dark and shelter had not yet taken over. October 2025 is the blank column. Only a few items were priced that month from administrative sources — new vehicles, used cars and gasoline among them — so only those rows carry a contribution through it. A near-white cell anywhere else is a small contribution, not a missing one: the hole is the column blank in every other row.

Table 34Where the level actually wentIndex, 5 years ago = 100

Price levels, rebased five years back.

All itemsCoreCore goodsCore services (dashed)

Rates answer “how fast”; this answers “how far”, which is the question people are actually asking when they say prices are high. Rebased inside the window, so every line starts at 100 five years ago rather than at its own base year — the published indexes sit on four different bases and are not comparable as levels. Table 35What seasonal adjustment removesPercent, month on month

Monthly CPI with and without adjustment.

Seasonally adjustedUnadjusted

The same index twice, as monthly changes: the published unadjusted series and its seasonally adjusted form. Over twelve months the two are nearly identical — seasonality cancels — so every other chart here can use either. Month to month they are not remotely the same, and the unadjusted line is the one that would have you reading January as a shock every year.

Table 36What revises, and what does notIndex points, first published to latest

Only the seasonally adjusted CPI revises.

Seasonally adjustedUnadjusted

Payroll figures are revised because more survey responses arrive. The CPI is not revised at all in that sense — the published index is final the day it prints. The unadjusted line here has not moved on a single month since January 2021; it sits exactly on zero, which is the point of drawing it. What does move is the seasonally adjusted index, on 59 of the 66 months from January 2021 to June 2026, because BLS recalculates seasonal factors each January across the prior five years. So an SA revision is a change to the adjustment, never to the price data underneath — and this year’s months read zero until next January reaches back and touches them.

Table 37What the typical price is doingTwelve-month change, %, unadjusted

The median price change against the mean.

Median itemHeadlineCore

Headline and core are weighted means, and a mean is what a few violent items carry. This is the median: half the basket by expenditure share rising faster, half slower. The difference is the point — headline peaked near 9% in mid-2022, the median at 8.1% in April 2023, ten months later, and it has fallen far more slowly since. Core removes food and energy by assumption; the median sets an item aside only for being at the edge this month. Built on a 135-item partition of the unadjusted index whose weights sum to 98.5 of 100; October 2025 is blank because the CPI was barely collected that month. Analyst-derived.
