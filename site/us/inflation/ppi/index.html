Final demand, month on monthLess foods, energy and trade, month on month

Producer prices, and how a cost shock travels from the raw end of the economy to the finished one — which turns out to be a story about how much of it survives the journey rather than how long the journey takes.

Table 1Final demand, and what is left after strippingPercent, year over year, s.a.

Final demand, then two core measures.

Final demandLess foods and energyLess foods, energy and trade

Producer prices are what firms receive, before retail margins and before tax — so this is the cost side of the CPI, not a competitor to it. The three lines are successive strippings: the headline, then less foods and energy, then less foods, energy and trade services. That last one is the PPI’s own core, and the extra removal matters here in a way it has no analogue in the CPI: trade services measure retail and wholesale margins, which move when the gap between what a distributor pays and charges moves, not when any price does.

Table 2Final demand, month and yearPercent, s.a.

Bars are the month; the line is the year.

Month on monthYear over year

Bars are the monthly change, the ink line the twelve-month change on the same axis. Producer prices are far noisier month to month than consumer prices — there is no retail smoothing between the shock and the print — so single months here deserve even less weight than they do on the CPI page.

Table 3Goods, services and buildingPercent, year over year, s.a.

Final demand in its three parts.

GoodsServicesConstruction

Final demand splits three ways. Services are roughly two-thirds of it by weight and goods a third, with construction a small remainder — the reverse of the intuition that a producer price index is mostly about factories. Goods carry nearly all the volatility, because goods are where energy sits.

Table 4What final demand is made of, yearPercentage points, 12-month change

Five parts that add up to the annual rate.

Final demand (line)ServicesGoods less foods and energyFoodsEnergyConstruction

Not rates — contributions: each part’s own change multiplied by its share of final demand, so the five add up to the headline instead of merely sitting beside it. A complete partition, weights summing to 100.000%: services 68.338, goods less foods and energy 18.515, foods 5.452, energy 5.061, construction 2.634. The ink line is final demand itself, so any daylight above the stack is the residual, shown rather than claimed away. Services carry roughly two-thirds of the weight, which is why a large move in goods often barely shifts the headline.

Table 5And what the month is made ofPercentage points, 1-month change

The same five parts, monthly.

Final demand (line)ServicesGoods less foods and energyFoodsEnergyConstruction

The identical decomposition monthly, same colours and same order, so a column reads across from the chart beside it. This is the pair to check when the two directions disagree — a month can add to the monthly rate while the annual rate falls, because the month dropping out of the window twelve back was larger still. Energy is the part that most often does it.

Table 6And what each is worthPercent of final demand

Each part’s share of final demand.

The weights behind the two charts above, and the reason their shapes look as they do. Published as relative importance in PPI news release Table 1, dated December 2025, and re-drifted with relative prices each month by the same rule the CPI page uses, so these are current rather than a frozen table. Trade services alone are 19.8% of final demand — a distributor’s margin, not a price of anything — which is why the PPI’s own core strips them out as well as foods and energy, and why its core is a different construction from the CPI’s.

Table 7The chain, yearPercent, year over year, s.a.

Four production stages leading to final demand.

Final demandStage 1Stage 2Stage 3Stage 4

The production-flow system, which exists to answer exactly this question. Output from stage 1 is an input to stage 2, and so on to stage 4, whose output is an input to final demand. Read it as one wave passing through four filters. The stages move together, and the thing that changes as you go down is the size of the swing, not its timing — see Table 9.

Table 8The chain, monthPercent, month on month, s.a.

The stages move in the same month.

Final demandStage 1Stage 2Stage 3Stage 4

The same five monthly, and this is where a transmission lag would show if there were one. Measured on this data there is not: correlating each stage’s monthly change against final demand’s at leads of nought to six months, every stage peaks at nought — stage 1 at +0.78, stage 3 at +0.81, stage 4 at +0.83, all contemporaneous. A cost shock upstream does not take months to reach the finished end. It arrives the same month, smaller.

Table 9How much of a shock actually arrivesIndex, 5 years ago = 100

Swings shrink down the chain.

Final demandStage 1Stage 2Stage 3Stage 4

The transmission, drawn as distance travelled rather than rate. If costs passed through whole, these five lines would end together; the gap between the top and the bottom is the share of the upstream move that never reached the finished price. Measured over 2010–2026, the standard deviation of the twelve-month change falls from 6.80 at stage 1 to 2.69 at final demand, and the extremes compress from a range of −9.3% to +22.4% down to −1.5% to +11.6%. Downstream prices also almost never fall — final demand has a floor around −1.5% where stage 1 reaches −9.3%. Note stage 2 is the most volatile of all, not stage 1: it carries the most energy and metals, so the chain is not monotone at the top.

Table 10The chain over four yearsPercent, 12-month change, 48 months

Each stage’s yearly change over four years.

The chain as a grid, reading downward: stage 1 at the top, final demand at the bottom. A vertical band of colour is a shock hitting every stage in the same month, which is what the correlations say happens; a diagonal would be the lagged pass-through people expect and that this data does not show. Two things are worth staring at. Late 2022 is dark across every row at once. Then through 2023 the top three rows turn red while the bottom two never do — the raw end of the economy went through outright deflation that the finished end simply did not experience, which is the same asymmetry the ranges in Table 9 measure. The scale saturates beyond ±12%, which stages 1 to 3 spend much of 2021–22 outside.

 The chain, month by monthPercent, 1-month change, 48 months

Each stage’s monthly change over four years.

The same five stages and row order as Table 10, on the one-month change instead of the twelve-month one. This is where timing shows: a shock that reaches every stage in the same month is a single dark column here, and a lagged pass-through would be a diagonal. Stage 2 swings hardest month to month, as it does over the year, and final demand least. The scale is ±2%, a sixth of Table 10’s, and more than nine readings in ten sit inside it.

Table 11The volatile threePercent, year over year, s.a.

Foods, energy and trade margins.

FoodsEnergyTrade services

The components the PPI’s core strips out, and the reason it strips them. Foods and energy behave as they do in the CPI, only more so, because there is no retail margin absorbing the move. Trade services are the odd one: a margin, not a price, so it rises when distributors widen their spread and falls when they compress it — which tends to happen at the turn of a cycle rather than with costs.

Table 12Services, with and without marginsPercent, year over year, s.a.

Services with margins and freight taken out.

ServicesServices less trade and transportTrade servicesTransport and warehousing

Final demand services taken apart: trade 19.8% of final demand, transportation and warehousing 4.9%, and everything else 43.7% — which is the whole 68.3%. When the headline services line diverges from the stripped one, the move is margins and freight rather than the price of services being sold, a distinction with no counterpart in the CPI, where a margin is invisible inside the shelf price. This is the most useful panel here for telling a genuine services-cost move from a distribution one.

The other cut of intermediate demand: instead of asking how far along a good is, this asks what kind it is. Unprocessed goods are the rawest thing the index measures and swing hardest; processed goods are damped; services for intermediate demand barely move by comparison. Same underlying prices as Tables 7 and 8, sorted a different way.

Table 14The long runPercent, year over year, s.a.

Raw against processed goods over twenty years.

Unprocessed goodsProcessed goods

Processed and unprocessed goods are the only part of this framework with a long history — they run from 1947, where everything else here starts in November 2009, because the Final Demand–Intermediate Demand system only launched in 2014. Twenty years of window rather than five, to show that the amplitude gap between raw and processed is a permanent feature of the data and not an artefact of the post-2020 episode.

Table 15Fuel and materials, yearPercent, year over year, s.a.

Gasoline, diesel and lumber.

GasolineNo. 2 dieselLumber

The three commodity inputs that most often decide what final demand goods did. Gasoline is the recurring one: a single month of it routinely accounts for most of the move in final demand goods. Diesel is separated from it deliberately: it is the freight cost, moving with distillate rather than motor spirit, so the two diverge exactly when transport costs matter most. Lumber is the housing input, and the only one here with sixty years of history. These are commodity indexes: they sit inside final demand rather than summing to it, so they are drawn as rates and never stacked.

 Fuel and materials, monthPercent, month on month, s.a.

Gasoline, diesel and lumber, monthly.

GasolineNo. 2 dieselLumber

The same three monthly, and this is the scale the headline actually reacts to — a single month of gasoline routinely moves several percent, which is why final demand goods is the noisiest line on this page. Diesel and gasoline mostly move together; the months they do not are refinery or distillate events rather than crude ones.

Table 16Freight and travel, yearPercent, year over year, s.a.

Freight against airline fares.

Transportation of freight and mailAirline passenger services

Two services that turn with the cycle rather than with costs. Transportation of freight and mail is the cleanest cyclical signal the release carries — haulage and delivery are priced on spot capacity, so they react before almost anything else. Airline passenger services is the most volatile services line in the index, and unlike most of the PPI it is something households buy directly. Both are seasonally adjusted, so they read against each other and against everything else on this page.

 Freight and travel, monthPercent, month on month, s.a.

Freight and airline fares, monthly.

Transportation of freight and mailAirline passenger services

Monthly, freight is the one to watch: it sits close to a spot price, so it turns months before the annual rate beside it admits anything has changed. Fares swing harder in any given month but carry less about the cycle: they follow jet fuel and capacity decisions rather than demand for haulage.

Table 17What the PCE deflator takes from here, yearPercent, year over year, s.a.

Health care prices that feed PCE.

Physician careHospital inpatient care

The reason to read PPI detail at all. These feed the PCE price index directly — the measure the Federal Reserve actually targets — because the national accounts source much of health care from producer prices rather than from the CPI. That is why a PPI print can move the PCE forecast on the day even when the CPI has already landed: the CPI measures what households pay at the counter, the PCE picks up what insurers pay, and this is where the second number comes from. Both are seasonally adjusted, so they read against each other and against everything else on this page.

 What the PCE deflator takes from here, monthPercent, month on month, s.a.

Health care prices that feed PCE, monthly.

Physician careHospital inpatient care

Both are quiet enough that the scale had to be redrawn to show them. Physician care has not moved a full percent in a single month once in five years — its median monthly step is 0.10% — and hospital inpatient care, livelier at 0.27%, has crossed 1% three times in sixty months. Health care is surveyed on a slower cycle than most of the index and much of it reprices on annual contracts, so a near-flat month is the normal state rather than an absence of news, and the occasional step is a contract resetting rather than a market moving. That is why the annual rates beside them are what forecasters carry into a PCE estimate.

Table 18Portfolio management, yearPercent, year over year, not adjusted

Fees set by asset values, unadjusted.

Portfolio management (NSA)

Given its own axis for two reasons, not one. It is published unadjusted only — there is no seasonally adjusted version at all, unlike the health care series above — so it sits on a different basis and must not be read against them month to month. And it swings between roughly −15% and +35%, which on a shared axis flattens everything else into a line.

It matters anyway: it is the other large PCE input taken from producer prices, it is routinely the largest single mover of final demand services, and because it is driven by asset values rather than by costs it can pull measured inflation around for reasons that have nothing to do with the price of anything.

 Portfolio management, monthPercent, month on month, not adjusted

Portfolio fees, monthly and unadjusted.

Portfolio management (NSA)

Read this one with care. The series is unadjusted, so a monthly change carries the season as well as the signal — management fees are struck on asset values at period ends, and much of the shape here is that billing calendar rather than anything about prices. The scale is the giveaway: the median monthly step is 1.79% against 0.10% for physician care beside it, and it has run between −7.1% and +8.1%, crossing one percent in 46 of the last 60 months. It is shown because the contrast is instructive — this is what every other monthly chart on the site would look like before seasonal adjustment. For a judgement about direction, use the twelve-month line beside it, where the season cancels.

Table 19Where the level wentIndex, 5 years ago = 100

Price levels, rebased.

Final demandGoodsServicesConstruction (dashed)

Rates answer how fast, this answers how far. Rebased inside the window, because the published indexes sit on several different base years and are not comparable as levels. Goods and services diverge permanently here in a way they do not in the rate charts: a period of fast goods inflation leaves a step in the level that later disinflation does not undo.

Table 20How much the numbers move afterwardsIndex points, first published to latest

How much PPI revises after first print.

Final demandStage 1

A third kind of revision, different again from the other two dashboards. Payroll figures move because more survey responses arrive; the CPI index never moves at all and only its seasonal adjustment does. PPI moves because late reports arrive, and it moves a lot: 120 of the 139 months from January 2015 to July 2026 were revised, by a median of 0.13 index points and by as much as 0.78. The timing is bimodal — of the months that moved, 50 first changed one month after first print and 52 at four months, the latter being the single scheduled revision BLS documents — but the two groups are alike in size, so what differs is when rather than how much. The ink line is stage 1, which revises slightly harder than final demand does, in keeping with everything else on this page.

Breadth rather than level: a final demand index held up by one violent component and one held up by a general rise read the same on the headline.
