Producer prices, and how a cost shock travels from the raw end of the economy to the finished one — which turns out to be a story about how much of it survives the journey rather than how long the journey takes.

Table 1Final demand, and what is left after strippingPercent, year over year, s.a.

Producer prices are what firms receive, before retail margins and before tax — so this is the cost side of the CPI, not a competitor to it. The three lines are successive strippings: the headline, then less foods and energy, then less foods, energy and trade services. That last one is the PPI’s own core, and the extra removal matters here in a way it has no analogue in the CPI: trade services measure retail and wholesale margins, which move when the gap between what a distributor pays and charges moves, not when any price does.

The production-flow system, which exists to answer exactly this question. Output from stage 1 is an input to stage 2, and so on to stage 4, whose output is an input to final demand. Table 8The chain, monthPercent, month on month, s.a.

The same five monthly, and this is where a transmission lag would show if there were one. Measured on this data there is not: correlating each stage’s monthly change against final demand’s at leads of nought to six months, every stage peaks at nought — stage 1 at +0.78, stage 3 at +0.81, stage 4 at +0.83, all contemporaneous. A cost shock upstream does not take months to reach the finished end. It arrives the same month, smaller.

Table 9How much of a shock actually arrivesIndex, 5 years ago = 100

The transmission, drawn as distance travelled rather than rate. If costs passed through whole, these five lines would end together; the gap between the top and the bottom is the share of the upstream move that never reached the finished price. Measured over 2010–2026, the standard deviation of the twelve-month change falls from 6.80 at stage 1 to 2.69 at final demand, and the extremes compress from a range of −9.3% to +22.4% down to −1.5% to +11.6%. Downstream prices also almost never fall — final demand has a floor around −1.5% where stage 1 reaches −9.3%. Note stage 2 is the most volatile of all, not stage 1: it carries the most energy and metals, so the chain is not monotone at the top.

Table 10The chain over four yearsPercent, year over year, 48 months

The chain as a grid, reading downward: stage 1 at the top, final demand at the bottom. A vertical band of colour is a shock hitting every stage in the same month, which is what the correlations say happens; a diagonal would be the lagged pass-through people expect and that this data does not show. Two things are worth staring at. Late 2022 is dark across every row at once. Then through 2023 the top three rows turn red while the bottom two never do — the raw end of the economy went through outright deflation that the finished end simply did not experience, which is the same asymmetry the ranges in Table 9 measure. The scale saturates beyond ±12%, which stages 1 to 3 spend much of 2021–22 outside.

Table 11The volatile threePercent, year over year, s.a.

The components the PPI’s core strips out, and the reason it strips them. Foods and energy behave as they do in the CPI, only more so, because there is no retail margin absorbing the move. Trade services are the odd one: a margin, not a price, so it rises when distributors widen their spread and falls when they compress it — which tends to happen at the turn of a cycle rather than with costs.

Table 12Services, with and without marginsPercent, year over year, s.a.

Final demand services taken apart: trade 19.8% of final demand, transportation and warehousing 4.9%, and everything else 43.7% — which is the whole 68.3%. When the headline services line diverges from the stripped one, the move is margins and freight rather than the price of services being sold, a distinction with no counterpart in the CPI, where a margin is invisible inside the shelf price. Table 15Fuel and materials, yearPercent, year over year, s.a.

The three commodity inputs that most often decide what final demand goods did. Gasoline is the recurring one — the July release attributes more than half of the entire fall in final demand goods to it, and that is a normal month rather than a remarkable one. Diesel is separated from it deliberately: it is the freight cost, moving with distillate rather than motor spirit, so the two diverge exactly when transport costs matter most. Lumber is the housing input, and the only one here with sixty years of history. These are commodity indexes: they sit inside final demand rather than summing to it, so they are drawn as rates and never stacked.

Table 16Freight and travel, yearPercent, year over year, s.a.

Two services that turn with the cycle rather than with costs. Transportation of freight and mail is the cleanest cyclical signal the release carries — haulage and delivery are priced on spot capacity, so they react before almost anything else — and at +8.5% over the year to July 2026 it is running well ahead of final demand as a whole. Airline passenger services is the most volatile services line in the index, up 11.9% on the year, and unlike most of PPI it is also something households buy directly. Both are seasonally adjusted, so they read against each other and against everything else on this page.

 Freight and travel, monthPercent, month on month, s.a.

Monthly, freight is the one to watch: it sits close to a spot price, so it turns months before the annual rate admits anything has changed — the +3.9% jump in April 2026 was visible here long before the twelve-month line beside it responded. Both series turned down in July, freight by 0.7% and airline fares by 3.4%. Fares swing harder in any given month but carry less about the cycle: they follow jet fuel and capacity decisions rather than demand for haulage.

Table 17What the PCE deflator takes from here, yearPercent, year over year, s.a.

The reason to read PPI detail at all. These feed the PCE price index directly — the measure the Federal Reserve actually targets — because the national accounts source much of health care from producer prices rather than from the CPI. That is why a PPI print can move the PCE forecast on the day even when the CPI has already landed: the CPI measures what households pay at the counter, the PCE picks up what insurers pay, and this is where the second number comes from. Both are seasonally adjusted, so they read against each other and against everything else on this page.

 What the PCE deflator takes from here, monthPercent, month on month, s.a.

Both are quiet enough that the scale had to be redrawn to show them. Physician care has not moved a full percent in a single month once in five years — its median monthly step is 0.10% — and hospital inpatient care, livelier at 0.27%, has crossed 1% three times in sixty months. Health care is surveyed on a slower cycle than most of the index and much of it reprices on annual contracts, so a near-flat month is the normal state rather than an absence of news, and the occasional step is a contract resetting rather than a market moving. That is why the annual rates beside them are what forecasters carry into a PCE estimate.

Table 18Portfolio management, yearPercent, year over year, not adjusted

Given its own axis for two reasons, not one. It is published unadjusted only — there is no seasonally adjusted version at all, unlike the health care series above — so it sits on a different basis and must not be read against them month to month. And it swings between roughly −20% and +25%, which on a shared axis flattens everything else into a line.

It matters anyway: it is the other large PCE input taken from producer prices, it led the whole of final demand services in July at +6.5%, and because it is driven by asset values rather than by costs it can pull measured inflation around for reasons that have nothing to do with the price of anything.

Portfolio management (NSA)

 Portfolio management, monthPercent, month on month, not adjusted

Read this one with care. The series is unadjusted, so a monthly change carries the season as well as the signal — management fees are struck on asset values at period ends, and much of the shape here is that billing calendar rather than anything about prices. The scale is the giveaway: the median monthly step is 1.79% against 0.10% for physician care beside it, and it has run between −7.1% and +8.1%, crossing one percent in 46 of the last 60 months. It is shown because the contrast is instructive — this is what every other monthly chart on the site would look like before seasonal adjustment. For a judgement about direction, use the twelve-month line beside it, where the season cancels.

Table 20How much the numbers move afterwardsIndex points, first published to latest

A third kind of revision, different again from the other two dashboards. Payroll figures move because more survey responses arrive; the CPI index never moves at all and only its seasonal adjustment does. PPI moves because late reports arrive, and it moves a lot: 120 of the 139 months since 2015 were revised, by a median of 0.13 index points and by as much as 0.78. The timing is bimodal — of the months that moved, 50 first changed one month after first print and 52 at four months, the latter being the single scheduled revision BLS documents — but the two groups are alike in size, so what differs is when rather than how much. The ink line is stage 1, which revises slightly harder than final demand does, in keeping with everything else on this page.

Breadth rather than level: a final demand index held up by one violent component and one held up by a general rise read the same on the headline. Counted, not weighted — Table 1 is the only PPI table with relative importances, and its finest split leaves a single component worth 34% of the basket, so a weighted median would mostly be that one series. Counting is how the payroll diffusion indexes work.
