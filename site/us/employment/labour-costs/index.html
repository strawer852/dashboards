Labour Costs

What labour costs per unit of what it produces, and whether that is where inflation is coming from. Pay rises are only inflationary net of productivity, and that subtraction is the spine of this page. Two releases feed it and they publish about a week apart — the Employment Cost Index at the end of October, Productivity and Costs in early November — so one half is routinely a week fresher than the other. The stamp above follows Productivity.

Table 1Unit labour costsNonfarm business, % change

What an hour of labour costs an employer for each unit it produces. This is the number that connects wages to prices: if pay rises are matched by output per hour, unit costs do not move and there is no pressure on prices from labour at all. The annualised line is what the news release leads on and it is far noisier — a single quarter of it has swung more than five points inside a year. Read the twelve-month line for direction.

Table 2Pay less productivityNonfarm business, twelve-month change, %

The whole of Table 1 in one subtraction. Unit labour costs are hourly compensation minus productivity, and this is an identity rather than an estimate — measured across every quarter here the two sides agree to a tenth of a point, which is rounding. It is the most useful chart on the page because it says which of the two moved. Pay decelerating and unit costs falling is a labour market cooling; productivity accelerating with pay unchanged is the same fall in unit costs for an entirely different reason.

Table 3The Employment Cost IndexAll civilian workers, twelve-month change, %

The cleanest wage measure published, because it prices a fixed job mix. If a recession removes low-paid jobs, average pay rises without anyone getting a rise; the ECI holds occupation and industry weights still so that cannot happen. Benefits are the half that is usually ignored and they do not track wages — health insurance and retirement move on their own schedule.

Table 4Three wage measures disagreeTwelve-month change, %

Three respectable measures of the same thing, and they routinely differ by more than a point. Average hourly earnings is the one quoted on the day and the one most easily misread: it is a simple average, so it rises when low-paid jobs disappear and falls when they come back — which is why it spiked in 2020 while nobody received a rise. Hourly compensation from the productivity release includes benefits and employer taxes and is revised heavily. The ECI holds the job mix fixed. When they disagree, the mix is usually why. Note that earnings is monthly and the other two are quarterly.

Table 5What is pushing prices upNonfinancial corporations, twelve-month change, %

The question this page exists to ask. Nonfinancial corporations is the only sector for which BLS splits the price of output into what it costs to make and what is kept, because it is the only one where profits can be measured against output. Here are the price of output and the labour half of its cost; the non-labour half is Table 6, drawn apart because it swings four times as wide and flattens these two into a band. The deflator is a share-weighted combination of the three components, roughly sixty per cent labour, and the shares move — so these are lines rather than a stack, which would assert a partition that does not hold to the decimal.

Table 6The non-labour sideNonfinancial corporations, twelve-month change, %

The other two components of Table 5’s deflator: everything that is not labour, split into what it costs and what is kept. Both range from about −20% to +50% where unit labour costs stays inside ten points, which is why they are here and not there. Read them against Table 5, because the question is only ever comparative — whether the price of output is moving because of what it costs to produce or because of what is retained. Unit profits is the most heavily revised series on this page, estimated late and restated afterwards, so a single quarter of it should not carry an argument.

Table 7The labour shareNonfarm business, index 2017 = 100

The share of what is produced that goes to the people who produced it. Drawn over the full history back to 1947 deliberately, because the interesting thing about this series is not the quarter — it is that the level sat roughly flat for thirty years and then fell through the 2000s and has not recovered. Published as an index rather than a percentage, so read the shape and not the number.

Table 8Private against governmentTotal compensation, twelve-month change, %

State and local government pay is set by budget cycles and bargaining agreements rather than by hiring competition, so it turns late and it turns slowly. That lag is visible in both directions: government trailed badly through the 2021–22 acceleration and then kept rising after private pay had begun to cool.

Table 9Wages against benefitsPrivate industry, twelve-month change, %

Benefits are about thirty per cent of what an employer pays and they are driven by something else entirely — chiefly health insurance premiums and pension funding. A gap opening between these two lines means the cost of employing someone is moving for reasons that have nothing to do with the labour market.

Table 10Wage growth by industryPrivate industry, twelve-month change in total compensation, %

Where the pressure actually is. A heatmap rather than lines because there are ten rows and the palette stops at six — the colour order is what makes the charts readable without colour vision, so a seventh line would have to reuse a slot. Goods-producing and service-providing are the two aggregates and the rest sit inside them, so the rows are not a partition and are not meant to add up.

Manufacturing productivity is measured against physical output and is far more volatile than the business-sector aggregate; durable goods swings hardest of all. The business and nonfarm business lines differ only by farms and sit almost on top of each other, which is a useful check that nothing has gone wrong rather than a finding.

Table 13Output and hoursNonfarm business, twelve-month change, %

Table 2 one level down. Productivity is output divided by hours, so these two lines decompose it the same way pay and productivity decompose unit labour costs — and the distinction matters, because output per hour rises for two very different reasons. Output growing faster than hours is an economy producing more. Hours falling faster than output is an economy shedding labour, which raises measured productivity while nothing improves. The 2020 spike is the second kind and is the clearest example in the series.

Table 14Real hourly payNonfarm business, twelve-month change, %

Hourly compensation after inflation: whether an hour of work buys more than it did a year ago. Below zero for most of 2021 and 2022, which is the single clearest statement of what that inflation did to households, and the reason wage growth that looks high in Table 3 did not feel like it.

Table 15Labour costs against core inflationTwelve-month change, %

The question the whole page exists to answer. Unit labour costs are what the Federal Reserve watches for evidence that inflation has become self-sustaining through wages. The two are related but the relationship is loose and the lead is not reliable in either direction — this is drawn so the gap can be seen, not because one predicts the other. Core PCE is monthly and lives on the PCE dashboard.
