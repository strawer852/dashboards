Weekly Claims

The highest-frequency read on the labour market. Published every Thursday, five weeks ahead of the payroll report that covers the same period.

Table 1Initial claimsPersons, s.a.

New applications for unemployment insurance. Weekly data is noisy, so the four-week average is the series to read.

Table 2Continuing claimsPersons, s.a.

People still claiming after the first week. Rising continuing claims against flat initial claims means the newly unemployed are taking longer to find work.

Table 3Insured unemployment ratePercent, s.a.

Continuing claims as a share of covered employment — the claims equivalent of the unemployment rate, and unaffected by changes in labour force participation. Against the household survey’s own rate, the gap is the share of unemployed people drawing no benefit: not covered, exhausted, or never having claimed. That gap is why claims can sit at historic lows while payroll growth stalls.

Table 5Claims against payroll growthIndexed, 5 years ago = 100

Both indexed to the start of the window so turning points line up rather than levels. Payrolls are monthly on a weekly axis, so the line is carried through the intervening weeks. Claims led payrolls for decades, but that relationship has broken down: the correlation between the insured claims rate and payroll growth ran −0.83 through the 2000s and is near zero in the 2020s. Layoffs stayed historically low while hiring stalled, and claims only ever see the first of those.

Table 6What the figure was when it was first publishedPersons, current less first print

Two revisions sit in this one line, and they are different orders of magnitude. The quiet recent stretch is the weekly correction from state agencies reporting late: the median has been exactly +1,000 in each of the last four years, about half a percent of the level, and since 2023 it has changed the direction of the week-on-week move in only 6% of weeks. On its own it says nothing. The excursions further back are the other one — once a year, in late March or early April every year since at least 2010, the seasonal factors are re-estimated and between 140 and 260 past weeks are restated at once. Each year’s re-estimation lands on top of the last, so the older a figure is the less it resembles what was published at the time: weeks three to five years old now sit a median 16,000 from their first print, 7.5% of the level, against 0.5% for the past year. Fifty of the fifty-two weeks of 2019 carry a different value than the one first published. A figure noted down today is not the figure that will be here next year. The line stops in 2009, where the vintage archive begins, and breaks again in the autumn of 2025, when four weeks were not published until 20 November — where nothing was published on time there is no first print to compare against, and unknown is not zero.
