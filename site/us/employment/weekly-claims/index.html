Weekly Claims

The highest-frequency read on the labour market. Published every Thursday, five weeks ahead of the payroll report that covers the same period.

New applications for unemployment insurance. Weekly data is noisy, so the four-week average is the series to read.

Table 2Continuing claimsPersons, s.a.

People still claiming after the first week. Rising continuing claims against flat initial claims means the newly unemployed are taking longer to find work.

Table 3Insured unemployment ratePercent, s.a.

Continuing claims as a share of covered employment — the claims equivalent of the unemployment rate, and unaffected by changes in labour force participation. Against the household survey’s own rate, the gap is the share of unemployed people drawing no benefit: not covered, exhausted, or never having claimed. That gap is why claims can sit at historic lows while payroll growth stalls.

Table 5Claims against payroll growthIndexed, 5 years ago = 100

Both indexed to the start of the window so turning points line up rather than levels. Payrolls are monthly on a weekly axis, so the line is carried through the intervening weeks. Claims do not lead payrolls, and measurement says they never did. Correlating the insured unemployment rate against twelve-month payroll growth at leads of nought to six months, the peak sits at nought in every era — −0.66 in the 1970s and 80s, −0.78 in the 1990s, −0.96 in the 2000s. What claims give you is not a lead but timeliness: weekly, and three to four weeks before the payroll print covering the same month. The relationship has also weakened badly — −0.67 in the 2010s — and since 2021 it has changed sign, running +0.60 rather than near zero, almost certainly because the insured rate and payroll growth both fell together as the reopening faded rather than because one now predicts the other. Sixty-two months is not enough to call that a relationship. Layoffs stayed historically low while hiring stalled, and claims only ever see the first of those.

Table 7How many of the unemployed actually draw benefitsContinuing claims as a share of total unemployment

Continuing claims divided by the unemployment level — the recipiency rate. It is the number that decides how much of the labour market this dashboard can see at all: when it falls, claims describe a shrinking share of the unemployed and every reading here becomes a narrower measure. Five years rather than ten: in 2020 and 2021 the emergency programmes pushed insured unemployment above the measured unemployment level, and on a longer axis that peak flattens everything else into the floor. The numerator is weekly and the denominator monthly, so the monthly value is carried forward across the weeks that follow it rather than matched exactly. The break in late 2025 is real: the household survey published no unemployment level for October 2025, and a missing denominator is left as a gap rather than bridged. Analyst-derived.

Table 8How broad is the rise in claims?Share of 53 states and territories, %

Every state compared with itself a year earlier, and the chart is the share standing higher. This answers what the national total cannot: whether a rise is general or the work of two large states. One state doubling moves the national figure and leaves this untouched; forty states drifting up moves this and may barely show nationally. Above 50 more states are rising than falling. States publish claims unadjusted only, which is exactly why the comparison is with the same week a year before — that carries the same seasonal position, so no adjustment is needed.

Table 9Initial claims by stateChange on a year earlier, %, last 52 weeks

The breadth measure above, shown as where. Each row is a state against its own level a year before, so the seasonality that dominates unadjusted claims is differenced out. Read it for columns rather than cells: a single dark row is one state’s plant closure or storm, while a column darkening across many rows is the thing the national figure is about to show.

Table 10How far claims sit above their own floorPer cent above the trailing 52-week low

The level of claims that counts as high has drifted down for forty years as covered employment changed shape, so the usual reading is a rise off the cycle low rather than a level. Measured, it is a poor warning. At the 1990, 2007 and 2020 business-cycle peaks this stood at +9%, +9% and +10% against a median week of +6% — it did not distinguish those months from an ordinary one. Only 2001 was different, at +36%. It rises steeply after a downturn starts, which makes it a confirming measure and not a leading one. Shown because how far claims sit off their floor is worth knowing; captioned so it is not mistaken for a signal. Analyst-derived.

Table 6What the figure was when it was first publishedPersons, current less first print

Two revisions sit in this one line, and they are different orders of magnitude. The quiet recent stretch is the weekly correction from state agencies reporting late: the median has been exactly +1,000 in each of the last four years, about half a percent of the level, and since 2023 it has changed the direction of the week-on-week move in only 6% of weeks. On its own it says nothing. The excursions further back are the other one — once a year, in late March or early April every year since at least 2010, the seasonal factors are re-estimated and between 140 and 260 past weeks are restated at once. Each year’s re-estimation lands on top of the last, so the older a figure is the less it resembles what was published at the time: weeks three to five years old now sit a median 16,000 from their first print, 7.5% of the level, against 0.5% for the past year. Fifty of the fifty-two weeks of 2019 carry a different value than the one first published. A figure noted down today is not the figure that will be here next year. The line stops in 2009, where the vintage archive begins, and breaks again in the autumn of 2025, when four weeks were not published until 20 November — where nothing was published on time there is no first print to compare against, and unknown is not zero. Analyst-derived from the vintage history.
