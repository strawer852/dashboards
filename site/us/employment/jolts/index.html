Table 3Vacancies per unemployed personRatio, s.a.

Openings divided by the number of unemployed. Above one, there are more vacancies than jobseekers — the tightness measure the Fed watches.

Table 4Beveridge curveRates, s.a.

The vacancy rate against the unemployment rate, traced in time order. Movement along the curve is the cycle; a shift of the whole curve is a change in how well the labour market matches people to jobs.

Table 6Separations, in fullMillions, s.a.

Quits and layoffs are the two everyone watches, but they do not add up to separations on their own. Other separations — retirements, transfers between locations, deaths — runs at a few hundred thousand a month and is what closes the total. Reading quits and layoffs alone against hires therefore leaves a gap that looks like a data problem and is not.

Table 7Does JOLTS agree with the payroll survey?Thousands per month, 12-month average

Hires less total separations is what JOLTS says the month’s net employment change was; the establishment survey counts it directly. Two independent measures of one quantity, and they agree on average — the mean gap over 308 months is about 4,000 a month on series that move by hundreds of thousands. Month to month they do not agree at all: the standard deviation of the gap is roughly 936,000, so both sides are shown as twelve-month averages. This is a consistency check over a year, never a read on a single month. Analyst-derived.

Table 8Job openings by industryChange in openings, thousands, 48 months

The twelve supersectors sum to total nonfarm across all 308 months with a worst residual of 3k on an average of 5,510k — rounding, which is how you know none is missing. Four of them — mining and logging, information, financial activities and other services — are published by BLS but not carried by FRED, so they arrived through the second source and carry no revision history. Monthly change rather than the level, because the scale diverges around zero.

Table 9Quits rate by industryPer cent of employment, ten years

The quits rate is the closest thing JOLTS has to a measure of worker confidence: people leave when they believe they can be hired elsewhere. The page shows it for total nonfarm only, which hides the finding — the level differs more across industries than it does across the cycle. Leisure and hospitality runs near four per cent where government runs under one, and no aggregate conveys that. Twelve small multiples rather than twelve lines on one axis: past six categories the palette stops, and a rate is a level, so each is ink.

Table 11What kind of separation?Millions per month, ten years

Table 6 draws these as levels, which means the reader has to divide in their head to answer the question that matters: when separations rise, are people leaving or being let go? Stacked, the mix is the chart. Quits have run as high as 73.3% of all separations (April 2022) and as low as 17.3% in April 2020, when layoffs briefly took nearly everything — a swing no level chart shows. Five years rather than ten: that April 2020 bar reaches 15.9m against a normal 5.5m, and on a ten-year axis it flattens every other month into the floor. Stacking is honest here because the three parts are a partition: they reconcile to total separations with a worst residual of 1k on an average of 5,715k across 91 months. The ink line is that total, so any daylight is rounding.
