Table 2Breakeven employment growthThousands per month, s.a.

Unemployment holds steady when employment grows with the labour force, so the gain required is the labour force’s own growth net of the share that stays unemployed — an accounting identity, not a forecast. The labour force is shrinking, so breakeven has fallen through zero and a negative payroll print can still sit above it. Two windows are drawn because the estimate is sensitive to how far back it looks, the monthly change carrying sampling error of several hundred thousand; it is negative on both. January is excluded as a population-control level break rather than a flow, and the line breaks where the October 2025 household survey was not collected. Breakeven is on the household concept while payrolls count jobs, so the comparison is indicative. Analyst-derived.

Table 9Breadth of the payroll changeDiffusion index, 50 = balance

The heatmap above splits fourteen supersectors; these indexes count roughly 250 private industries, scoring each span as the share expanding plus half the share unchanged. Above 50 more industries are adding than shedding. It answers a question the headline cannot: whether a move is broad or the work of a few sectors. Table 11Who is unemployedThousands, s.a.

The headline rate says how many; this says what kind. Permanent job losers rising is structural displacement. Temporary layoffs are transient and reverse. Job leavers quit voluntarily, so a falling count is workers judging that they cannot find better — the household survey’s read on confidence. These are independently seasonally adjusted and do not sum to total unemployment.

Table 13Why the employment ratio movedPoints, change over 12 months

The employment-population ratio is participation multiplied by the share of the labour force in work, so its twelve-month change separates exactly into the two. Through the 2021–22 recovery the move came mostly from unemployment falling; the decline since is almost entirely participation, which makes it demographic and behavioural rather than a sign of rising joblessness. The gap between the ink line and the two components is the interaction between them. Analyst-derived.

Table 15Wage growth on measures that hold the job mix fixedPercent, year over year

Average hourly earnings move when the composition of employment moves: shed low-paid jobs and the average rises without anyone getting a raise. The employment cost index holds the occupational mix fixed, and the Atlanta Fed tracker follows the same individuals over time, so both answer the question average earnings only appears to. Different releases: the ECI is quarterly from BLS and the tracker is monthly from the Atlanta Fed — neither is dated by the stamp at the top of this page, and the ECI holds its level for three months at a time because that is how often it is published.

Table 16Pay against productivityPercent, year over year

Wage growth is only inflationary to the extent it outruns productivity, and the difference is what unit labour costs measure — the published series, not a subtraction done here, because average hourly earnings and nonfarm business compensation are different concepts over different sectors. Real hourly earnings are BLS’s own CPI-deflated series. Different releases again: productivity and unit labour costs are quarterly, and real earnings are published with the CPI, so on payroll release morning the newest month is not yet there.

Table 18The two surveysIndex, 5 years ago = 100

The establishment survey counts jobs; the household survey counts people, and a second job counts once. BLS publishes a research series that restates household employment on the payroll survey’s own concept — removing agriculture, the unincorporated self-employed and unpaid family workers, and netting out multiple jobholding — which is the like-for-like line to read against payrolls. All three break at October 2025, when the household survey was not collected.
