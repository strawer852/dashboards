Table 2Breakeven employment growthThousands per month, s.a.

Unemployment holds steady when employment grows with the labour force, so the gain required is the labour force’s own growth net of the share that stays unemployed — an accounting identity, not a forecast. Table 13Who is unemployedThousands, s.a.

The headline rate says how many; this says what kind. Permanent job losers rising is structural displacement. Temporary layoffs are transient and reverse. Job leavers quit voluntarily, so a falling count is workers judging that they cannot find better — the household survey’s read on confidence. These are independently seasonally adjusted and do not sum to total unemployment.

Table 14Slack beyond the headlineThousands, s.a.

U-6 counts three groups where U-3 counts one, and these are them. Involuntary part-time workers hold a job but want full-time hours, so they never appear in the unemployment rate at all. The marginally attached want work and have looked in the past year but not the past four weeks, which puts them outside the labour force by definition. Both move earlier than unemployment in a downturn: hours are cut and search is abandoned before anyone is counted as unemployed.

Table 15How long people stay unemployedWeeks, and percent of the unemployed

Duration separates a labour market where people move quickly between jobs from one where they cannot get back in. Median weeks is the typical spell; the share unemployed 27 weeks or more is the tail that does not clear, and long spells erode re-employment prospects on their own. Two units, so two axes — weeks on the left, percent on the right. The share is the published figure, not one divided out of two rounded levels.

Table 16Why people are unemployedMillions, s.a.

A complete partition of unemployment, which is why it stacks: everyone unemployed is a job loser, a job leaver, a reentrant or a new entrant. The ink line is total unemployment, so the daylight above the stack is the residual — about 33,000 in August, from the four being seasonally adjusted independently of the total. The composition matters more than the level: job losers rising is displacement, while reentrants rising is people being drawn back in, which is a different economy entirely.

Table 26Multiple jobholdersPercent of employed, s.a.

The share of employed people holding more than one job — the cleanest measure of the gap between counting jobs and counting people. The establishment survey counts someone with two jobs twice; the household survey counts them once, which is part of why the two surveys diverge in the panel further down. A rising share means payroll growth overstates how many people are working.

Table 30Labour input, not headcountPercent, year over year

Headcount is the coarsest measure of how much work is being bought. Aggregate weekly hours combines employment with the length of the workweek, and aggregate weekly payrolls adds the hourly wage, which makes it the monthly read on nominal labour income — the money actually reaching households. Temporary help is the most disposable headcount a firm holds, so it is cut before permanent staff and hired before them in a recovery.

Table 32Manufacturing hours and overtimeHours per week

A factory adjusts hours before it adjusts people: overtime is withdrawn first, then the workweek shortens, then employment falls. That ordering is why manufacturing hours feed the Conference Board’s leading index.
